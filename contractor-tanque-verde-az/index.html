Building in the Tanque Verde Valley

Tanque Verde sits on Tucson's far east side, in a valley framed by the Santa Catalina Mountains to the north and the Rincon Mountains to the east. Tanque Verde Creek and Agua Caliente Creek run through it, and the name itself comes from a large stock water tank, a "green tank", from the ranching days of the late 1800s. Most addresses here are Tucson 85749.

The area became a Tucson suburb by the 1960s, but it never lost its rural feel. Larger lots and horse properties are common, much of the land carries covenants that limit how densely it can be built, and residents tend to value the open desert as much as the house itself. That makes Tanque Verde one of the better parts of the metro for a custom home, a detached guest house or a serious addition.

Who issues permits in Tanque Verde

Tanque Verde is not a city or town. It's an unincorporated community in Pima County, so building permits come from Pima County Development Services, not the City of Tucson. In the county, a detached casita is permitted as a guest house: one per lot, it may have a kitchen, it must share the main home's driveway and access, and it can't have its own utility meter.

Two more checks matter here more than in most of the metro:

  • Washes and creeks. With two creeks and plenty of smaller washes, many lots touch a regulatory floodplain or erosion hazard area. In unincorporated Pima County, nearly any improvement in those areas, including additions, walls, fences and pools, needs a floodplain use permit before work begins.
  • Private covenants. County zoning is only part of the picture. Recorded covenants on many Tanque Verde lots can set their own limits on what you build and where. We ask for them early.

Septic, utilities and long driveways

Many larger county lots are on septic. Adding a bedroom, a primary suite or a guest house can mean the system has to be checked or enlarged before a permit is issued, under Pima County's onsite wastewater rules. Distance matters too: the farther a guest house sits from the main panel, water line and septic tank, the more trenching it takes. We walk all of that on the site visit so the estimate reflects your actual lot.

Designing for the views and the desert

On a big Tanque Verde lot, where the house sits matters as much as what it looks like. We look at the high ground and drainage paths first, then at the views toward the Catalinas and Rincons, the afternoon sun and how far utilities have to travel. A house or guest house that steps with the land usually costs less in grading and drainage work than one that fights it. Deep roof overhangs and covered porches on the south and west sides cut heat gain, and a matching stucco and roof palette helps a new guest house or addition look like it was always part of the property. Those choices are easier to get right on paper than to fix later, which is why we walk the lot before anything is drawn.
