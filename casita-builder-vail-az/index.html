Casita builder in Vail, AZ

Guest houses and family suites on Vail and Corona de Tucson lots, permitted through Pima County and planned around septic, wells and washes.

Vail casitas go through Pima County

Vail is not an incorporated town. Most Vail addresses, and Corona de Tucson to the south, are unincorporated Pima County, so a casita here is permitted by Pima County Development Services. The county calls a detached casita a guest house, and its rules are different from the City of Tucson's ADU rules you may have read about.

The guest house basics

Per the county's guest house rules, a guest house is a detached building with at least two rooms and a bathroom, and it may have a kitchen. One is allowed per lot, on lots of 4,000 sq ft or more. It follows the accessory building setbacks for your zone, sits at least 3 feet from the main home, shares the main home's driveway and can't have its own utility meter. Occupancy is limited to family members and their non-paying guests.

Vail also has plenty of larger rural lots. On those, the county's zoning code has a separate secondary dwelling option in the RH and GR-1 zones for an ill, disabled or elderly person who needs care, or for their care provider. That permit is valid for up to three years and can be renewed. It is a different application from a guest house, so we confirm which one fits your situation.

Septic: the first thing to check in Vail

Many Vail homes aren't on sewer. If yours is on septic, the system has to handle the casita too. Pima County must issue a Construction Authorization before a septic system is built or replaced, and a Discharge Authorization after the final field inspection before it can be used. A site investigation decides whether a conventional or alternative system is needed. Adding a bedroom and bathroom can mean the existing system needs work, so we look at the tank location, the disposal field and the casita's placement together on the first visit.

Tip: before you settle on a spot for the casita, find your septic tank and disposal field. Building over or too close to them is one of the most common reasons a casita has to move.

Wells, washes and native plants

  • Wells. Some Vail lots run on a private well. A domestic well extension usually just means plumbing, but a new or deepened well needs a Notice of Intent to Drill filed with the Arizona Department of Water Resources and a licensed well driller.
  • Washes and floodplains. Nearly any structure in a regulatory floodplain or erosion hazard area needs a Floodplain Use Permit before construction. Vail has plenty of washes, so we check the county's flood maps first.
  • Native plants. The county's native plant rules apply once grading reaches 14,000 sq ft or a grading plan is required. A casita alone rarely gets there, but a long new access path on a big lot can.
  • Caliche. Hard soil layers are common and can slow the foundation dig.

Distance is the other Vail cost. Larger lots tempt people to put the casita far from the house for privacy, but a guest house shares the main home's meter and driveway, so every extra foot means more trenching for power and water and a longer path to keep up. Somewhere between close enough to be practical and far enough to feel separate is usually the sweet spot, and we can sketch options on the site walk.

How we build a casita in Vail

  1. Site walk. Lot, access, septic, well, washes and how you'll use the space.
  2. County check. Guest house or secondary dwelling, zone setbacks, floodplain and septic steps.
  3. Plans. Drawings and the site plan for the county's custom home process. Our casita floor plan guide helps with layouts.
  4. Permits. Building permit plus any septic or floodplain approvals.
  5. Build. Our own crew does the framing, structural work, roofing and stucco.
  6. Inspections and final. We schedule inspections and walk it with you.
