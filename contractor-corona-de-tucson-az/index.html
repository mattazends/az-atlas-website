Building and remodeling in Corona de Tucson

Corona de Tucson sits southeast of Tucson at about 3,300 feet, toward the Santa Rita foothills and south of Vail, in the 85641 ZIP code. It's served by the Vail Unified School District, which is a big part of why it has grown so fast. The census counted 813 residents in 2000, 5,675 in 2010 and 9,240 in 2020, so most of the homes here are less than 25 years old.

That shapes the work. Very few Corona de Tucson projects are about saving an old house. Most are about making a newer one fit the family better: a bedroom suite for a parent, a guest house, a covered patio that actually blocks the sun, or upgrades to builder-grade bathrooms and kitchens.

Who issues permits in Corona de Tucson

Corona de Tucson is a census-designated community, not an incorporated town, so it falls under unincorporated Pima County. Permits come from Pima County Development Services, not the City of Tucson, even if your neighbor a few miles north in Rita Ranch deals with the city.

That difference matters for casitas. Arizona's 2024 ADU law covers cities over 75,000 people, so the City of Tucson's ADU rules don't apply here. Instead, a detached casita is a county guest house: one per lot, on a lot of at least 4,000 sq ft, kitchen allowed, sharing the main home's driveway and access, and with no separate utility meter. If your neighborhood has an HOA, its review usually comes before the county permit.

Additions and guest houses on newer homes

Newer homes are often the easiest to add on to, because the framing, roof and stucco are in good shape and the plans may still be on file. The main questions are space and utilities:

  • Attached addition. A primary suite or mother-in-law suite that shares a wall can tie into the existing systems and keeps the yard open.
  • Detached guest house. More privacy, but it needs its own foundation, roof and utility runs. Our casita vs. addition guide compares the two.
  • Septic or sewer. If your lot is on septic, adding a bedroom can mean the system has to be checked or enlarged first under Pima County's onsite wastewater rules.
  • Drainage. Lots near a wash may need a floodplain use permit before anything is built, including walls and patio slabs.

Outdoor space and builder-grade upgrades

Many newer homes come with a small slab patio and little shade. A solid patio cover or pergola, framed and roofed to match the house, changes how much of the year you can use the yard. Inside, the common requests are walk-in showers in place of tub combos, better kitchen cabinetry and storage, and built-ins. See our bathroom and custom cabinet pages.

Even newer homes need care. Stucco can crack at control joints and around windows as the house settles, and roof flashings and penetrations are the first places to leak in monsoon storms. Our own crew handles stucco and roofing repairs, small or large.

How a project runs here

  1. Site walk. We look at the lot, the house, the utilities and any wash nearby, and confirm it's county jurisdiction.
  2. Design and HOA. Plans, materials and colors, submitted to your HOA first if you have one.
  3. County permit. We prepare and submit to Pima County Development Services and answer review comments, plus any septic or floodplain review.
  4. Build. Foundation, framing, roofing, stucco and interiors with our own crew on the structural and exterior work.
  5. Inspections and final walk. We schedule county inspections and walk the finished work with you.
