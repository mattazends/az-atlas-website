Casita vs home addition: which should you build?

Both add space. They solve different problems. Here's how to tell which one is yours.

Plan an Addition or Casita Call

The one-line answer

If the space is for your own household, a home addition usually works better. If it's for someone who needs their own front door, like a parent, a grown child, guests or a tenant, a casita usually wins. Everything below is about the cases where it isn't that simple.

What each one is

A home addition extends the existing house: a bedroom, a primary suite, a family room or a bigger kitchen, all under one roof and on one cooling system or an added mini-split. A casita is a small second home on the lot with its own bathroom and usually a kitchen. In the City of Tucson it's an ADU (attached or detached). In unincorporated Pima County a detached one is a guest house.

Side by side

Privacy and independence

A detached casita gives both households their own door, kitchen and quiet. An addition joins your daily life; it's part of the house. An attached ADU or mother-in-law suite sits in between.

Construction and cost drivers

An addition's big costs are the tie-ins: opening an exterior wall, joining a new roof to the old one, extending plumbing and cooling, and matching stucco. A detached casita skips most tie-ins but needs its own foundation, four walls, a roof and longer utility runs. Neither is always cheaper. See the home addition cost guide and casita cost guide, and for a whole-house comparison the custom home cost guide.

Size limits

Inside the City of Tucson, a casita is capped at 75% of the main home's floor area or 1,000 sq ft, whichever is smaller, under HB 2720. An addition isn't an ADU, so it's limited by your zone's setbacks, lot coverage and height instead. If you need a lot of space, that can tip the decision.

Utilities

Additions usually tie into existing lines. A casita needs trenching for sewer or septic, water and power. Pima County guest houses can't have separate utility meters (county rules).

Permits

Both need a building permit. A casita also has to meet the ADU or guest house rules for your jurisdiction, and many HOAs review both. Our casita rules guide explains who issues what.

Renting it out

Inside the City of Tucson, state law requires that an ADU can be rented long term; if one built after the 2024 law is a short-term rental, the owner has to live on site. A Pima County guest house is meant for family and non-paying guests. An addition is just part of your house.

Yard and lot

A detached casita uses more yard and needs room for setbacks on all sides. An addition can be more compact. On a tight lot, a garage conversion or a second-story addition may be the only way to add space.

Resale

Both add value when they're permitted and look like they belong. A casita appeals to buyers who want space for family or rental income. An addition appeals to buyers who simply want a bigger house. Unpermitted or mismatched work hurts either.

Quick decision guide

  • Bigger primary bedroom and bath for you: addition.
  • Parent who needs daily help: attached suite or attached ADU.
  • Independent parent or adult child: detached casita.
  • Rental income (City of Tucson): casita as an ADU.
  • Visiting family a few times a year: detached casita or county guest house.
  • No yard to spare: garage conversion or second story.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Who will use the space, and for how long: years, or a few weeks a year?
  • Do they need their own kitchen and front door, or just a bedroom and bath?
  • Would you ever want rental income from it?
  • How much of the yard are you willing to give up?
  • Will anyone using it need step-free access now or later?

Honest answers to those five questions usually make the choice obvious.
